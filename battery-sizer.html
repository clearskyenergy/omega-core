Peak kW is the billed demand on the invoice. Enter the demand rate that actually applies to it — add facilities + distribution + transmission $/kW together if they are separate line items, and gross it up for sales tax if the utility charges it.
Bills give totals, not shape. The curve is now built inside the on-peak window on the worst day — the only hours that can set the determinant, and the day the battery actually has to survive. Base load is read off the on-peak kWh where the tariff is known; raise it for a flat site, lower it for a spiky one. This is the single biggest assumption in the model and the reason interval data is worth asking for.
2 Rate
A subscription is a step function, not a slope. Shaving 40 kW off a 503 kW peak saves nothing unless it crosses a block boundary, and nothing at all below the floor. Enter the block price from the current tariff sheet — these escalate, and a stale number is the whole error.
Ratchet 0 = none. At 80%, billed demand never drops below 80% of the highest peak in the prior 11 months — which caps what a battery can recover in year one. Headroom oversizes the final rating against load growth and metering error.

4 Audit someone else’s model
Paste the headline numbers off a vendor proposal. Every claim gets checked against what the hardware can physically do and against the same energy budget — a battery cannot spend the same kWh twice.
Subscription check: if the site sits on a PG&E BEV or SDG&E EV-HP rate, switch step 2 to subscribed blocks before auditing. A 503 kW shave off a 600 kW peak is 10 blocks of 50 kW — real money, but roughly a tenth of what the same shave is worth on a $20/kW demand charge.
5 Engineering design
Sizing says how many kW and kWh the bill justifies. This says what that system is — containers, converters, transformer, breakers, cable, fault duty, CTs — and whether it is buildable. Size the battery first and these fields fill themselves; every one of them is still yours to override.
